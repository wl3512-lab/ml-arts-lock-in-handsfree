I'm adding a Teachable Machine model, trained on my own desk, to the study timer I already use every day, so it starts focus when I sit down, pauses when I walk away, and dims when I pick up my phone.

Abstract

lock in is a study timer I use every day. It started as my friend Braxton Hauw's web timer, and I built a Mac menu bar version with themes, background sounds and share codes so two people can study on the same clock. It works well, but it only knows what I tell it. I have to remember to press start, which is the hardest part of studying for me. I forget to press pause when I get up, so "25 minutes of focus" often includes ten minutes in the kitchen. And it has no idea when I've been on my phone for a while.

For the final I'm giving it eyes, under one rule: the machine learning can only remove a step, never add one. No typing in what I'm studying, no rating sessions, no new screens. I'll train an image model in Teachable Machine on my own desk with three classes (working, on my phone, away) and load it with ml5.js. When I sit down, a soft five-second "starting…" begins focus. When I've been away for ten seconds it pauses and takes those seconds back. When I've held my phone for twenty seconds the ring quietly dims. No popups and no sounds.

Most of the work is in how it feels when the model is wrong: one tap undoes a wrong pause and gives the time back, a quick stretch shouldn't pause anything, and a camera that watches you study has to feel like help, not surveillance. I'll also compare my trained model against a pretrained one to see when training your own is worth it.

Why train my own model? This is the week 8 question. A pretrained person detector like bodyPose sees "a person" whether I'm working or scrolling, so it can tell away from here but not phone from work. Teachable Machine gives me transfer learning: MobileNet already knows how to see, and I only teach it what my desk looks like in three states. That takes about 50 photos per class and a few minutes. I'll run both side by side for a day and count the mistakes each one makes, so the answer comes from my own numbers.

Inspiration

Mostly my own week: I keep finding sessions that say I focused for 50 minutes when I know I didn't. Then a few references:

  • Calm technology. Mark Weiser and John Seely Brown's "Designing Calm Technology" (1995) describes technology that stays in the periphery and only moves to the center when needed. That's the feel I want: you notice the ring dim, nobody nags you.
  • iPhone's attention-aware features. The phone keeps the screen on while you look at it and lowers alert volume when you do. It's ML you never configure, and it only removes steps.
  • Focus apps like Forest. They work by making you press a button and punishing you for leaving. I want the opposite: no button, and no punishment.
  • Teachable Machine (week 2). It's the reason this is small enough to build. Anyone can retrain it on their own desk in a few minutes.

Audience

First, me, as an audience of one. I'll use it for my real studying for the next three weeks. Then three friends who already study with me on lock in share codes, each training it on their own desk.

The goal is that people barely interact with it. You sit down, and it's already counting. The only interactions are corrections: canceling a start you didn't want, or tapping "I was here" after a wrong pause. Every correction gets counted, so I can measure whether it's actually saving steps.

Challenges

  • Phone vs. work. A phone is a small object, and I sometimes have it on the desk while working. I'll train with the phone in my hand vs. lying on the desk, in daylight and at night.
  • Timing. React too fast and stretching pauses the timer. React too slow and it feels broken. Tuning the hold times is design work, not just code.
  • Shared sessions. Today, pausing in lock in changes the share code, which would break a friend's session. In a shared session it should only track my away time and never move the shared clock.
  • Trust. It's a camera pointed at you while you study. It's off by default, a small eye shows when it's on, frames are never saved or sent anywhere, and one switch turns it off.

Code sketch

The sketch already runs the full start / pause / dim logic. Paste a Teachable Machine model link to use the camera, or use demo mode with keys 1, 2 and 3. The rules are in handsfree-logic.js, separate from p5 so they can be tested: 9 tests cover sitting down, low-confidence frames, a quick stretch, the phone, undoing a wrong pause and canceling a start.
